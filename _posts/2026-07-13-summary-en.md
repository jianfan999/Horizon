## [Near-light-speed electrons reshape chemical bonds seen firsthand](https://36kr.com/newsflashes/3894693500894465?f=rss) ⭐️ 9.0/10

A Brown University team directly observed how relativistic electrons moving at near-light speed alter the shape of chemical bonds in a bismuth-carbon charged molecule, as published in Science. This breakthrough provides the first experimental confirmation of relativistic effects on chemical bonding, challenging traditional models and paving the way for new understanding of heavy-element chemistry and materials design. The team used femtosecond spectroscopy to capture the real-time dynamics, revealing that spin-orbit coupling from relativistic electrons significantly weakens pi bonds while strengthening the sigma bond in the bismuth-carbon system.

rss · 36氪 · Jul 13, 23:27

**Background**: For heavy elements like bismuth, electrons near the nucleus can reach a significant fraction of the speed of light due to high nuclear charge, causing relativistic effects such as spin-orbit coupling. These effects alter electron orbitals and chemical bonding, but direct observation has been challenging. This experiment marks the first time such changes have been visualized experimentally.

This breakthrough brings perovskite-organic tandem solar cells closer to practical application by exceeding the 28% efficiency threshold, demonstrating that these next-generation photovoltaics can potentially surpass the theoretical limits of single-junction silicon cells. The advancement could accelerate the deployment of low-cost, high-efficiency solar technologies in renewable energy markets. The 28.04% efficiency was certified by an independent authority, and the device architecture combines a wide-bandgap perovskite top cell with a narrow-bandgap organic bottom cell. rss · 36氪 · Jul 13, 23:13

Tandem cells stack two or more absorbers with complementary bandgaps to capture a broader range of the solar spectrum, theoretically exceeding the efficiency limits of single-junction cells. Perovskite-organic tandem cells combine the strengths of both material classes and are considered a promising route to high-efficiency, scalable photovoltaics.
