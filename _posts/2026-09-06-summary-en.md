## [AMD BC-250 DIY Gaming PC Real Costs Spark Debate](https://devquasar.com/hardware/the-60-gaming-pc-amd-bc-250/) ⭐️ 6.0/10

A 2025 article promotes the AMD BC-250 board as a '$60 gaming PC,' highlighting how secondhand boards can be repurposed. Community comments dispute the price tag, noting the board now often costs over $150 and needs numerous extra parts. This story captures growing interest in repurposing formerly niche hardware for budget gaming and Linux PCs. It matters to DIY builders because the true cost and tinkering effort determine whether such projects are realistic alternatives to standard secondhand office desktops. One owner reports paying about $186 for a BC-250 board alone, plus needing a PSU, NVMe drive, high-pressure fan, DisplayPort-to-HDMI adapter, and a DIY case. A modified BIOS can unlock some chips from 24 to 40 GPU compute units and from 6 to 8 CPU cores, though results vary by board.

hackernews · networked · Sep 5, 13:36 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49576386)

**Background**: The AMD BC-250 is a low-cost board built around a modified AMD APU with Zen 2 CPU cores and RDNA 2 graphics, often described as repurposed PlayStation 5 processor silicon. These boards were originally used in cryptocurrency mining installations and later appeared on the secondhand market, making them attractive for hobbyist Linux gaming builds. Because many units have cores and graphics units disabled during binning, enthusiasts flash custom BIOSes to try to unlock the full hardware potential.

## [An Essay Argues LLMs Act as a 'Cognitive Virus'](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03344) ⭐️ 6.0/10

A new arXiv paper (2609.03344) argues that large language models act as a 'cognitive virus,' reshaping how people think and whether they delegate mental tasks. The piece is primarily a philosophical essay rather than a new technical breakthrough, and it has sparked community debate about the validity of the viral analogy. The essay reframes AI adoption from a purely instrumental question into a cognitive and cultural one, which matters for researchers, educators, and designers studying how automation changes human reasoning. It adds to a growing public conversation about the risks and benefits of offloading thought itself to AI. The paper explicitly says the viral analogy does not imply LLM-human interactions are intrinsically parasitic, since biological viruses can act as pathogens, mutualists, or evolutionary partners. The argument is conceptual, drawing on memetics and cognitive science, rather than being supported by new empirical experiments.

hackernews · canjobear · Sep 5, 20:02 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49580164)

**Background**: Memetics is the study of memes, which are units of cultural information that can be copied and spread through communication; the term was popularized by Richard Dawkins as the cognitive analogue of genes. The paper applies this lens to LLM-generated language, asking how AI-spawned ideas may propagate through human populations. Its concern echoes Socrates' famous argument that writing would 'implant forgetfulness in souls' by making people rely on external marks instead of internal memory. In this view, LLMs are not neutral tools but participants in cultural and cognitive evolution.

This marks one of the first times an AI-generated drama has reached mainstream broadcast television in China. This debut moves AI-generated video from social-media novelty to mainstream long-form entertainment, signaling that AI-produced dramas can be polished enough for prime-time audiences. It could reshape production economics in China's entertainment sector and accelerate industry adoption of generative AI. The drama is based on Journey to West, one of China's most famous classical novels, and was broadcast on state-owned Hunan Satellite Television. Until now, AI-generated video in China was mostly associated with 'AI slop'—low-quality, mass-produced clips circulating on social media.

**Background**: AI video generation uses text-to-video models to create clips from written prompts; systems like OpenAI's Sora, Google DeepMind's Veo 3, and Runway's Gen-4 can now produce footage nearly indistinguishable from real filming. Such models have mostly produced short clips, and low-effort mass-produced AI content—often called 'AI slop'—has flooded social media. A prime-time broadcast of a fully AI-generated drama is therefore a notable step toward long-form, professionally presented AI content.
